# Blend the split; the rings only move the pop

**Bottom line:** no setting of the existing distance rings can make the terrain stop popping, because a ring scheme's pop size and its vertex cost pull in opposite directions (pop height on screen falls as 1/C, vertices rise as C^2, so reaching the literature's 1-2 px "invisible" standard on canyon faces would need about 190x the current vertex load against a 28,000 guard), and the only published remedy that removes the pop without adding vertices or rewriting the renderer is a geomorph band: the new vertices a split creates start on the parent edge (height and Gouraud shade) and slide to their true values over a distance band, which is Röttger 1998's algorithm and the mechanism every modern heightfield renderer (CDLOD, geometry clipmaps, REDengine 3, O3DE, Unreal's non-Nanite Landscape) ships. The repo already has it built and switched off (`I76_TERRAIN_MORPH`, commit 610ef86). Coarser rings are worse, not better: fewer but larger pops, and more screen area changing per second. "One setting past a distance" is the one owner idea with real merit, but only as a two-level ladder (10 m to about 1 km, 20 m beyond) costing roughly twice R07's vertex peak and an estimated 1 % low near 60 fps, so it is a fallback, not the first move. "Ignore changes under a threshold" is exactly the screen-space-error criterion of Lindstrom, ROAM and chunked LOD, and the stock test already contains a height-error threshold; it is invisible today because a surface-type scan force-splits every tile of 40 m and larger at its ring regardless of error, and the threshold's distance law grows with d^2 instead of d, so it is strict near and loose far, the reverse of what the eye needs. Hysteresis, freezing and skirts do not apply: the lab's own trace shows every node flips once and never back (615 flips, 615 nodes), the shared-edge quadtree has no cracks to hide, and skirts would spend vertex indices. The recommended path is: turn the built geomorph on with a 30-50 % band, shade blended with the same factor, measure it with the lane's own per-vertex screen-jump log against the 1 px standard, add the flat-node split skip, and only then consider converting the d^2 threshold to a d^1 (pixel) one at the already-hooked node test.

## Your four questions, answered first

**"There are SO MANY rings; why wouldn't coarser be better?"** Because the ring positions and the pop sizes are tied together by one constant. A tile of size s splits when the eye comes within C x s of it (C = 18.3 on the x24 preset you drive, 3.7 on stock), and the vertex it adds moves on screen by roughly (K / C) x (delta / s) pixels, where delta is how far the new midpoint sits off the parent edge and K is the pixels-per-unit-slope constant of the display (about 1,250 at 1440 lines with a 60 degree vertical field, about 416 at 640x480; the game's real field of view has not been measured, so treat K as ±30 %). Making the rings coarser (smaller C) makes every pop bigger by the same factor it makes them rarer, so the total vertex displacement per second is unchanged, and the screen area that changes per second actually rises as 1/C because each popping tile is larger on screen. The classic papers measured this directly: Lindstrom found that a 1 px tolerance gives "virtually no popping" while every coarser setting trades fewer triangles for larger transitions, and Röttger states the vertex count grows "quadratically" with C ([Lindstrom et al. 1996](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr01/cs598b/papers/lindstrom96.pdf); [Röttger et al. 1998](https://otik.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)). Your own drives confirm the direction: stock x1 is the coarse end and "slope redraws aggressively, near terrain too"; x24 was "the first one with any improvement"; LOD 48 and A x96 were "further / better, the bad one still really bad" and "same behaviour" ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). Going finer shrinks the pops but cannot get them under the threshold of visibility: at the 28,000 guard the ring factor can reach about 35, which still leaves a canyon-face split (delta/s around 0.3) jumping about 10 px in one frame, and any one-frame jump of that size is a pop ([Scherzer & Wimmer 2008](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2008/SCHERZER-2008-FSR/SCHERZER-2008-FSR-draft.pdf)). There is "always a pop somewhere" because the rings are 2x apart in distance, one per tile size, so at any moment some size is crossing its ring; that is a property of the ladder, not of its scale ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)).

**"Or only one setting past a certain distance?"** A single level everywhere is the only ring-free option, and it is affordable in vertex indices but not in frame time. The finest tile is 10 m (the heightfield is 5 m, a node of half-size one sample). Within a 90 degree frustum, 10 m tiles out to 1 km come to about 7,900 vertices and 20 m tiles from 1 km to 2 km about 5,900 more, roughly 14,000 against R07's measured 7,458 peak and the 28,000 guard; the one remaining ring, at 1 km, pops by about K x delta / 1000 = 1.2 px per metre of delta, which on a 20 m tile under haze is at or below the visibility threshold. The price is the software vertex transform: the ladder showed the x24 split alone costing about 20 fps on the 1 % low (106 to 86) for roughly 3,000 extra vertices, so a crude linear read puts 14,000 vertices near a 60 fps 1 % low ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). The literature rejects the single far level for a different reason that also applies here: every modern engine states its goal as a constant screen-space triangle size (Frostbite: "Projected triangle size rougly constant"; Hoppe: "maintain triangles that are uniformly sized in screen space"; CDLOD: "the same average number of triangles per square unit of screen"), because that is where a fixed budget buys the most visible detail ([Frostbite GDC 2012](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc12-terrain-in-battlefield3.pdf); [Hoppe, GPU Gems 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf); [Strugar, CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD)). The one documented heightfield renderer that shipped a hard single boundary, the I26 Godot reimplementation of this very game, spent three releases fixing holes, hairline cracks on canyon walls and a backstop ring standing proud of the real ground, and tuned it by pushing the boundary out from 320 m to 450 m, which "reduced the frequency, not the mechanism" ([I26 releases](https://api.github.com/repos/vahokif/I26/releases)). So: a two-level ladder is the strongest form of your idea and a legitimate fallback if the geomorph disappoints, but it buys its stillness with about 20-25 fps on the lows.

**"Or changes under a certain threshold shouldn't just be ignored?"** Yes, and that is the standard answer: every classic and modern scheme is parameterised by a screen-space tolerance tau in pixels and splits only when the projected error exceeds it; 1 px is called "virtually no popping" (Lindstrom, Hoppe, de Boer), 2 px is the typical operating point (Lindstrom's fly-through, Hoppe's 1.7-2.1 px average, Ulrich's demo, Cesium's default), and 4-8 px is the cheap game setting ([Lindstrom et al. 1996](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr01/cs598b/papers/lindstrom96.pdf); [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf); [de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf); [CesiumJS Globe](https://cesium.com/learn/cesiumjs/ref-doc/Globe.html)). Two things stop the stock threshold from doing this job. First, it is bypassed: after a node passes the height-error test, any node larger than 20 m whose scan lines carry surface type 2, 3, 4, 6 or 7 (most of T05's dirt and paving) splits anyway, so the 40, 80 and 160 m tiles pop at their rings whatever B is set to, which is why `I76_TERRAIN_LOD_B 4` looked "exactly the same" to you ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md); [REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). Second, its distance law is wrong by one power: the stock allows a midpoint error of about B x d^2 / (2h) (in a power-of-two stair), whereas a constant pixel error allows delta proportional to d. The two laws cross at one distance; inside it the stock over-details, outside it the stock's on-screen error grows linearly with distance, so the far pops are the big ones ([Lindstrom et al. 1996](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr01/cs598b/papers/lindstrom96.pdf)). The important caveat: in the papers, "ignore changes below a threshold" is the split criterion itself, never a filter applied after a size rule has already asked for the split. A stateless rebuild that suppresses a split this frame and allows it next frame has merely delayed the same pop. The built `I76_TERRAIN_FLAT_SPLIT` is the one legitimate post-filter, because a node whose every sample lies within 0.3 m of its coarse surface changes nothing when it splits except the surface-type bookkeeping ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)).

**"How do modern games do this and why isn't a simple, cheap version applicable here?"** They use a distance-doubling ladder (the same 2x rings you have, chosen so each ring holds roughly constant screen triangle size), a vertex-shader geomorph over the outer 10-30 % of each ring (Hoppe's clipmaps n/10, CDLOD's "last 15 %-30 % of every LOD range", O3DE's 16 m of a 128 m first range), cracks closed by index stitching or by a morph that lands exactly on the parent mesh, and lighting from a normal map so geometry LOD never touches shading ([Hoppe, GPU Gems 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf); [Strugar, CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD); [O3DE terrain docs](https://docs.o3de.org/docs/user-guide/components/reference/terrain/world-renderer/)). The simple cheap version is applicable here, and that is the whole point of this report: the geomorph's height blend is pure arithmetic on data the CPU has in hand at the split (the two parent heights and the true height), CDLOD itself describes a CPU form for pre-shader hardware, and the proxy hooks the exact place (the transform at 0x490eed, the shade loop before 0x490f9b) where Röttger inserted it in 1998 on an SGI running a quadtree rebuilt every frame with no state ([Strugar, CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD); [Röttger et al. 1998](https://otik.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf); [TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)). What does not transfer is the per-pixel normal map (no texture-space lighting in this renderer), hardware tessellation and Nanite; the Gouraud analogue, blending the vertex shade with the same factor as the height, is in the built lane.

## What the stock split test actually does, corrected from the trace

The brief this report was written against described a forced ring at 12.9x the tile size. The geomorph lane's disassembly corrects that: the x87 stack at 0x4912fe..0x49136d multiplies a copy of (2h)^2 by the LOD constant, and the forced-split compare uses the unmultiplied value, so the stock forced split happens only when the eye is within **0.71 node sizes of the node centre** (the node under the car). The trace twin's decision model had placed the "E to N" band at 12.9x and credited it with 58.7 % of flips; those were the model's, not the game's, and the trace's own v1 lines (actual child visits) put forced splits at 2.9 % ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md); [REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). The real decision for a node of half-size h samples at squared eye distance d2 is therefore: beyond **(2h) x sqrt(K) = 18.3x the node size** on the x24 preset (3.7x on stock), a leaf, full stop; inside 0.71 sizes, a split; otherwise the five midpoint errors |h(mid) - mean(ends)| x 2h are tested against floor_pow2(d2) x B, and if all pass and the node is larger than 4 samples (20 m), the surface-type scan splits it anyway on typed ground ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)).

Three consequences follow. The "so many rings" are **183 / 366 m for 10 / 20 m tiles (height-error driven; steep ground has already saturated the B/24 threshold, so it splits the moment it is eligible, 30.7 % of flips) and 732 / 1,464 / 2,928 m for 40 / 80 / 160 m tiles (surface-type driven on T05's dirt and paving, flat or not)**, plus the err-step rings at 160 / 320 / 640 / 1,280 / 2,560 m where floor_pow2(d2) halves the tolerance (7.1 % of flips); merges are 4.1 % and the vertex guard refused nothing at a 7,458 peak ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). This is Röttger's flat-terrain floor "l < C d" with the roles inverted: his ring forces a minimum detail near the eye beneath an error-driven test, the stock ring caps detail at distance above one ([Röttger et al. 1998](https://otik.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)). Second, with B divided by 24 the error test is saturated on anything steep, so R07 behaves as a **pure distance-ring scheme**, which is precisely the configuration de Boer and Röttger describe as the source of block popping: "a preset (fixed) d for each GeoMipMap level will result in unwanted popping artifacts" and transitions "occur at fixed distances from the camera" ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)). Third, the pop size at a ring is uncorrelated with the ring: p ≈ (K / C) x (delta / s) with K / C ≈ 68 px on x24, so a rolling tile (delta/s ≈ 0.05) jumps about 3 px, a canyon face (delta/s ≈ 0.3) about 20 px, and a flat tile jumps 0 px in height but still changes its Gouraud shade because the new vertex's shade is computed from the heightmap around its sample (0x490fb0) rather than interpolated along the coarse edge, which is your "minor shade changes in flat tiles" ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)). The "back and forth" you see is not per-node flip-flop; the trace recorded 615 flips over 615 different nodes in the view centre of the hill leg. It is the same patch of ground changing at successive rings on approach (a 40 m tile becomes four 20 m tiles at 732 m, then those become 10 m tiles at 366 m, each time with a new fan diagonal snapping the slope shape) and merging again when you turn, so hysteresis and freeze "have nothing to act on", which the R05 / R06 / X2 drives confirmed ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)).

The resolution multiplies all of this. Reddy's thesis notes that distance-based thresholds "are no longer valid and must also be scaled appropriately" when display resolution changes, and the K factor makes it concrete: the same world-space split looks about three times larger in pixels at 1,440 lines than at the 480 lines the constants were tuned for, and the 1,800-2,000 m clip puts three more rings inside the view than the original 600 m fog did ([Reddy 1997](https://www.dcs.ed.ac.uk/home/mxr/thesis/thesis.pdf)). A world-space scheme that was tolerable in 1997 is not the same scheme today.

## Distance ring, screen-space error and morph compared

The three criteria differ in what they let through, and the table below is the comparison the brief asked for. "Ring" is the stock ladder as traced; "SSE" is the Lindstrom / ROAM / Ulrich / Cesium pixel criterion, rho = K x delta / D > tau; "morph" is a band applied on top of either.

| | Distance ring (stock, x24) | Screen-space error (tau px) | Geomorph band (on either) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What triggers a change | eye crosses C x s; typed ground ≥ 40 m splits regardless of error | projected error K delta / D exceeds tau | nothing new: the same split, but the new vertices start on the parent edge |
| Pop size on screen | (K/C)(delta/s): 3 px rolling, 20 px canyon, 0 px + shade flat | ≈ tau for every tile (rough tiles split earlier, flat ones later or never) | per-frame motion = pop x (v dt / band): 0.05-0.15 px per frame at 30 m/s for a 37-91 m band |
| Where pops cluster | in bands at the ring radii, all tiles on a ring at once | scattered, wherever a tile's error crosses tau | none visible; a tile in the band is slightly flattened |
| Vertex budget law | ∝ C^2, independent of terrain | ∝ roughness / tau^2; guard binds on canyons | unchanged (heights move, counts do not) |
| Flip-flop | none measured (615 / 615) | none in a stateless rebuild either; a tile at exactly tau alternates unless hysteresis or morph | removes the need: a crossing changes b by a few /255 per frame, not topology |
| Needs cross-frame state | no | no | Röttger: no (b from this frame's distance); the built lane: a per-vertex birth-distance table |
| Shading | new vertex gets its true shade in one frame | same | shade blended with the same factor (Hoppe 1998; clipmap normal blend) |
| Retrofit route here | constants A, B already patched; surface-type skip at 0x491556 | compute the decision in the node-test hook at 0x4912f6 (the trace stub) | built: 0x490eed, 0x490f9b, 0x490f82 (`I76_TERRAIN_MORPH`) |
| Literature home | Röttger's l < C d floor; de Boer's "preset d" (criticised) | Lindstrom 1996, ROAM 1997, Hoppe 1998, Ulrich 2002, Cesium | Röttger 1998, de Boer 2000, Ulrich 2002, CDLOD 2009, clipmaps 2004 |

The ring column's vertex law is what defeats scaling. Röttger ran C = 8 at about 15,000 vertices for a 1025 x 1025 field; the x24 preset's 18.3 is 2.3x his constant, which his quadratic rule prices at about 5x his per-frame load for the same terrain ([Röttger et al. 1998](https://otik.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)). The lab's numbers sit on that curve: R07 peaks at 7,458 vertices and LOD 48 (C x 1.41) at 9,609, so the 28,000 guard caps C near 35, at which a canyon split still jumps about 10 px ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). The SSE column fixes the allocation, not the pop: with tau at 4-8 px (the only range the budget is likely to afford on canyon ground) every split is a 4-8 px jump, which de Boer calls "occasional popping, especially when tau is set to a high value", and 1 px, where popping vanishes, makes "the probability of using a higher level GeoMipMap (lower detail) ... very low" ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)). That is why every author who measured it paired the criterion with a morph: Hoppe's geomorphs let him "increase the pixel error tolerance to improve frame rate (up to 72 frames/sec) with few noticeable artifacts", and de Boer's trilinear morph "totally eliminated" the popping his pixel criterion had only reduced ([Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf); [de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)). The morph column is therefore not an alternative to the other two but the component that makes either tolerable, and it is the only one of the three whose effect on the visible metric is independent of the vertex budget.

Converting the stock threshold to SSE is a real option because the node test is already hooked. The stock compares delta x 2h with floor_pow2(d2) x B; in pixel terms that is a tolerance tau(d) = (K B / 2) x (d / h), zero at the eye and equal to K x B x 18.3 at the ring, so the stock is strictly stricter than a constant-tau SSE everywhere inside a tile's ring and infinitely looser beyond it ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)). An SSE replacement computes split = (K_px x delta / d > tau) in the hook, pushes the ring beyond the clip, and relies on `I76_TERRAIN_FLAT_SPLIT` to neutralise the surface-type override; its vertex count then depends on T05's roughness, which no source can predict, so it is a measured experiment with the guard as the safety. de Boer's reminder that "pre-calculating D_n^2" is a cheap compare of a linear threshold, not a quadratic one, is worth keeping in view: the stock's d^2 is a genuine quadratic tolerance (floor_pow2 of d2 against the error), not a squared compare ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)).

## Which geomorph fits a quadtree with no memory

Seven geomorph variants are in the literature, and only one of them was designed for exactly this renderer. Röttger 1998 rebuilds the quadtree top-down every frame, decides each split by f = l / (d x C x max(c x d2, 1)), and morphs the up-to-five midpoint vertices a split creates with **b = 2(1 - f) clamped to [0, 1]**, "between the elevation of the lower level (which is the average of the two corresponding corner points) and the elevation of the higher level", storing b as one byte per node ("255 morphing steps, which is precise enough in practice") and taking the minimum of the two blocks' values on a shared edge so neighbours agree; the abstract claims it runs "at virtually no additional cost" at a fixed 25 Hz on a 250 MHz R4400 ([Röttger et al. 1998](https://otik.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)). de Boer's trilinear geomipmapping is the same quantity written as t = (d - D_n) / (D_{n+1} - D_n) applied to the odd vertices of a chunk; Ulrich's chunked LOD uses one per-chunk f with precomputed per-vertex deltas and skirts; CDLOD morphs each vertex by its own distance in the vertex shader, re-sampling the heightmap for the coarse height; clipmaps blend z = (1 - alpha) z + alpha z_c with alpha from the viewer's grid position over a band of n/10; and only Hoppe 1998 is time-based, with a per-vertex record (gtime, increment, backup geometry) that must survive across frames plus a velocity extrapolation of the viewpoint ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf); [Ulrich SIGGRAPH 2002](https://tulrich.com/geekstuff/siggraph02-slides/text26.html); [Strugar, CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD); [Losasso & Hoppe 2004](https://hhoppe.com/geomclipmap.pdf); [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)). Every variant except Hoppe's computes the factor as a pure function of the current camera distance, so none needs state between frames, and the cost ordering is Röttger (one lerp per morphing midpoint, f already computed for the split) < de Boer < Ulrich < CDLOD (an XY snap and a height re-fetch) < Hoppe. Wagner's measurement that CPU-side morphing dropped a geomipmap renderer from 504 to 312 fps is not a warning for this engine: his cost was re-uploading a vertex buffer that was otherwise static, and this renderer rewrites its entire terrain vertex pool every pass anyway ([Wagner 2003](https://www.ims.tuwien.ac.at/publications/tuw-138077.pdf)).

The built lane is Röttger's scheme with one deliberate difference. It names each vertex by its heightfield sample, derives its level from the lowest set bit of the sample coordinates, finds its two parents (always lower vertex indices, because an edge's ends exist before the edge splits) and writes y = lerp(mean(parents), true, t) walking up the indices so a chain of levels morphs continuously; the shade is blended with the same t after the stock shade loop; a shared edge is split once per pass so the midpoint has one value from both sides, which disposes of Röttger's min-across-edge step for free ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)). The difference is where t comes from: not Röttger's b = 2(1 - f) from the node's split ratio, but t = clamp((d0 - d) / band) with d0 the distance at which the vertex was born, kept in a per-vertex table across main passes. That buys two things the pure form lacks: a vertex that appears by other means (view edge, first pass, a camera jump over 40 m, turning the head) is mature at once, so the ground never shows flattened after a start or a teleport; and the merge direction morphs symmetrically, because driving away brings d back to d0 and t to zero before the node merges at the same ring. It costs the one byte per vertex Röttger also stored, in a hash the hysteresis code had already built. Whether the band should be stated as a fraction of d0 or in metres is the first tuning question, and the literature's guidance is consistent: Röttger's band is the whole half-range from the split distance to half of it (f in [0.5, 1]); CDLOD uses "the last 15 %-30 % of every LOD range" and warns that shrinking it "can make the transition between levels noticeable"; Losasso and Hoppe found n/10 works and "if w is much smaller, the level boundaries become apparent", while "if w is much larger, fine detail is lost unnecessarily"; O3DE ships 16 m of 128 m and recommends under 25 % ([Röttger et al. 1998](https://otik.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf); [Strugar, CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD); [Losasso & Hoppe 2004](https://hhoppe.com/geomclipmap.pdf); [O3DE terrain docs](https://docs.o3de.org/docs/user-guide/components/reference/terrain/world-renderer/)). The invisibility condition is per-frame motion under a pixel: a 20 px canyon pop spread over a 0.5 x 183 m = 91 m band at 30 m/s moves 0.055 px per frame at 120 Hz and takes 3 s; a 0.2 band (37 m) moves 0.14 px per frame over 1.2 s; both sit far under the 1 px standard, and doubling the speed doubles them. Start at 0.3-0.5; the lane's `I76_TERRAIN_MORPH_LOG` reports the largest per-vertex screen jump per pass, which is the number to hold under 1 px.

Cracks need no new work. Three families of fix exist in the literature: restrict neighbours to one level apart and give shared-edge vertices one agreed value (Röttger, de Boer's omitted edge vertex, Wagner's finer-follows-coarser), hide the gap with vertical skirts (Ulrich) or zero-area stitching triangles (clipmaps), or make the fully morphed fine mesh coincide with the coarse one (CDLOD) ([Ulrich SIGGRAPH 2002](https://tulrich.com/geekstuff/siggraph02-slides/text28.html); [Losasso & Hoppe 2004](https://hhoppe.com/geomclipmap.pdf)). This renderer already has the first family: an edge record is shared by both nodes and split once per pass, a midpoint against an unsplit neighbour is simply not drawn by that neighbour, and at t = 0 the morphed midpoint lies exactly on the coarse edge, which is de Boer's rule that such vertices "should not be multiplied by t" satisfied by construction ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf); [TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md)). Skirts would spend indices under a hard cap on geometry that is never seen, and would do so to solve a problem the engine does not have.

## The one modern component that transfers, and why the others do not

The modern stack is a geomorph band plus a constant-screen-size ladder plus texture-space lighting plus stitching; asked for the single component that applies here, the answer is the band, and each of the others fails a specific test. Screen-space error is the second most applicable and is feasible at the hooked node test, but it is a reallocation of vertices, not a pop remover, and its budget on canyon ground is unknown until measured; it is the step to take after the morph, when de Boer's logic lets tau be raised to buy frame rate without visible cost ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)). Hysteresis is a CPU decision with a documented width (Blender's game engine added a 10 % default "to avoid popping when a LOD object moves close to the LOD transition continuously"; Godot's visibility ranges use margins so LOD is not "switched back and forth quickly"), and the proxy already has it (`I76_TERRAIN_HYST`), but the trace shows nothing for it to damp and the R05 drive showed no improvement; it would only matter after an SSE conversion, where a tile sitting exactly at tau could alternate, and then a 10 % margin on the hooked decision is the fix, with the morph factor computed from whichever threshold is active for that node ([Blender BGE commit](https://projects.blender.org/archive/blender-archive/src/commit/1b71279adccb9f8cc1f4d7cbb3cbb06871da982e/source/gameengine); [Godot visibility ranges](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/visibility_ranges.html); [REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)). Temporal stability in the clipmap sense, "the terrain appears stable because the height data remains fixed in place", is a property this renderer already has: every vertex is a heightfield sample whose height never changes once it exists, so the instability lives entirely in creation events, which is exactly the surface the morph covers ([Terrain3D docs](https://terrain3d.readthedocs.io/en/stable/docs/introduction.html)). Skirts and stitching address cracks that the shared-edge quadtree does not produce. Normal maps and per-pixel lighting (Unreal stores the full-resolution slopes so "the entire high-resolution (non-LOD'd) normal data is available for lighting calculations"; clipmaps keep the normal map at twice the geometry resolution and blend it with the geometry's alpha) need a texture-space lighting path the engine lacks ([UDK Landscape docs](https://docs.unrealengine.com/udk/Three/Landscape.html); [Hoppe, GPU Gems 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)).

Frostbite is the useful counter-example: Battlefield 3 shipped with no geometry morph at all, relying on constant projected triangle size plus index-permutation stitching and accepting "Blocky crestlines (on console)" ([Frostbite GDC 2012](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc12-terrain-in-battlefield3.pdf)). That is the existence proof that fine enough geometry can ship without a morph, and the budget arithmetic above is why it does not apply at 28,000 vertices on a software transform. Fog is the last cheap component: it was the 1990s mask for the clip and Cesium still raises the screen-space error of tiles partly in fog and culls tiles fully in fog, with the caveat that making fog "more aggressive may cause a more noticeable 'popping' of the terrain tiles" because it is used to excuse coarser far geometry ([Cesium fog](https://cesium.com/blog/2015/11/12/fog/)). A haze band starting inside the outermost ring at 1,464 m is consistent with both eras, costs a constant, and covers whatever the morph leaves at the far ring; it is a cover, not a remedy, and the lab's own census already shows the far field is not where the visible change is (36 px/s beyond 300 m on stock against 4,088 within, and 605 beyond on x24 at 1,800 m) ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)).

## Shading pops: morph the shade, and the table of normals is already fine

No source claims that computing normals from the fine heightfield at every level removes the shading pop, and this renderer already does that: each new vertex's shade comes from the heightmap around its own sample, so coarse and fine vertices at the same sample agree, which is the vertex-level equivalent of Unreal's LOD-independent lighting ([TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21](../../records/TERRAIN-MORPH-2026-10-21.md); [UDK Landscape docs](https://docs.unrealengine.com/udk/Three/Landscape.html)). The pop is the time discontinuity: Gouraud was interpolating the two endpoint shades along the coarse edge, and the new midpoint arrives with its own fine shade in one frame, Wagner's case where "the removed normal produced a color value which was very different from its neighboring color values", which he found users "notice ... very easily" and solved by abandoning vertex normals for a lightmap ([Wagner 2003](https://www.ims.tuwien.ac.at/publications/tuw-138077.pdf)). The published remedies are three. Decouple lighting from geometry entirely (normal maps, baked lightmaps, the voxel engines' pre-shaded colour maps), which needs texture-space lighting and is out. Accept it: de Boer notes "a slight change in (gouraud) shading will occur. This sometimes can be spotted in real-time, but it is only slightly noticable", and his is the only judgement of its magnitude in the literature ([de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)). Or morph the attribute with the position: Hoppe 1998 interpolates "normal, color, and texture coordinates" during the geomorph ("Normals are interpolated over the unit sphere"), and the clipmap shader "looks up the normals for the current level and the next-coarser level and blends them using the alpha value computed in the vertex shader" ([Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf); [Hoppe, GPU Gems 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)). The CPU form on a fixed-function path is to start the new vertex at the mean of its parents' post-lighting shades and lerp to its own with the same t, which is pixel-identical along the split edge at t = 0 (a Gouraud rasteriser's value at the midpoint is exactly the endpoint mean) and is what the built lane does after the stock shade loop. What remains after that is de Boer's residual: the new fan diagonal changes the interpolation across the tile interior, a change that no vertex-attribute blend can remove and that the literature rates as slight. The one tunable worth knowing about is filter width: Hoppe found "one normal per vertex is too blurry" and a gamedev poster the opposite problem, coarse normals "biased towards the flat ground normals farther away", so a 5 m-sample shade will look rougher at 1,800 m than the coarse-level shade did, and if far ground reads as noisy after the morph is on, a wider shade filter for vertices born far away is the knob ([Hoppe, GPU Gems 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf); [gamedev.net topic 688460](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/688460-large-terrain-rendering-reducing-popping-and-calculating-normals/)).

The concrete order is this. Enable the built geomorph at a band of 0.3-0.5 of the birth distance with the shade blend on, drive the T05 hill leg and the canyon, and read the jump log: the pass criterion is a largest per-vertex jump under 1 px in every main pass at the route's speeds, and the owner's eye on the ridge behind the intro road, which is the OWNER-TEST-LIST row already written for it ([OWNER-TEST-LIST](../../OWNER-TEST-LIST.md)). If the ground reads as flattened on approach, narrow toward 0.2 (CDLOD's lower bound) rather than below it; if a transition still shows, widen toward Röttger's 0.5. Add `I76_TERRAIN_FLAT_SPLIT` at about 0.5 of the ring with the 0.3 m epsilon, which removes the surface-type splits that change nothing but shade on flat ground and returns some vertex budget. Keep A at x24: coarser is measurably worse and finer buys nothing visible once the morph is on. Then, with budget in hand, run the SSE experiment in the node-test hook at tau 4 px and compare vertex peak and the jump log against R07; if it holds under the guard on the canyon, raise tau until the lows recover, which is the trade de Boer and Hoppe describe and the morph makes invisible. Leave hysteresis and freeze off, do not add skirts, and add the haze band last if the 1,464 m ring still shows under the morph. Measure each step with the same instrument and n of at least 3, because the ladder's R00 to R03 step moved 9 fps for no identified reason and single runs of this engine have fooled the project before ([REBASELINE-2026-10-21](../../records/REBASELINE-2026-10-21.md)).

## Conclusion

The terrain's "redrawing" was never a tuning problem, which is why four knobs (A, B, hysteresis, freeze) and two owner drives produced one marginal improvement and three "exactly the same" verdicts: a stateless ring ladder with a saturated error test is a pure block-LOD scheme, the literature's canonical popping case, and its one free parameter trades pop size against vertex count along a curve that never reaches the eye's threshold under a 28,000-vertex software transform. The thing that changed this week is that the renderer turned out to be hookable at exactly the two places Röttger used in 1998, so the engine can be given the one component every modern terrain renderer has without adding a vertex or keeping more than a byte per vertex between frames.

What the owner should expect once it is on is not "fewer rings" but rings that no longer announce themselves: the same 40 events per second, each now spread over a second or three of driving, with the surviving residue being de Boer's slight interior-shading shift and whatever the far ring leaves at 1,464 m. The two ideas in his question that survive the analysis, a threshold in pixels and a single far level, are both real tools, in that order of usefulness, and both become cheaper after the morph has removed the pop they were being asked to hide.

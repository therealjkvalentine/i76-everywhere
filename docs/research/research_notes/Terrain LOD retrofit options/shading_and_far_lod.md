# Terrain LOD retrofit: shading pops at subdivision (Topic A) and the look of coarse far terrain (Topic B)

Scope: a 1997 MechWarrior 2 / Interstate '76-family renderer (5 m heightfield, quadtree rebuilt every frame, per-vertex Gouraud from vertex normals, no texture-space lighting, software transform, 32,767 vertices/frame, 640x480 + 600 m fog originally, now 1440p ultrawide with the view at 1800-2400 m). Facts about Interstate '76 itself are limited to what the brief states plus what the I26 project's own release notes say about the original; nothing else about I'76 is asserted.

Source-access note for the writer: the Röttger 1998, de Boer 2000, Strugar 2010, Hoppe 1998, Hoppe/Asirvatham GPU Gems 2, ROAM 1997, Scherzer 2008 and Reddy 1997 texts were downloaded as PDFs and read in full text (pdftotext); quotes from them are verbatim. The Wagner (ShaderX2) article, the gamedev.net thread, the Khronos thread and the UE4 Landscape page could not be fetched (403/JS-rendered) and are quoted only from search-result snippets; those are marked "(snippet)". Lindstrom 1996 could not be fetched at all.

## Key question A1: Does computing vertex normals from the fine heightfield at every LOD level remove the shading pop, and what do Röttger / de Boer / Strugar / Unreal / Unity say about lighting across LOD transitions?

### Takeaway
No primary source claims that fine-heightfield normals alone remove the pop; what the literature actually does is either (a) decouple lighting from the geometry LOD entirely (normal map / stored slopes sampled per pixel: Unreal Landscape, Strugar's demo, Hoppe's clipmaps, Wagner's lightmap) or (b) morph the normal/colour with the same blend factor as the height (Hoppe 1998 geomorphs, Hoppe/Asirvatham clipmap transition region). de Boer is the only one of the named papers that discusses Gouraud shading change at a connectivity change, and calls it "only slightly noticeable"; Röttger does not discuss lighting across levels at all.

### Cited Findings
- Röttger et al. 1998 describe the artefact being attacked as vertex popping, not shading: "With the viewpoint moving, the triangulation is continuously changing, resulting in a phenomenon called vertex popping. As the observer approaches an area with detail information, this detail will suddenly appear at a certain distance. To eliminate these artifacts we introduce a new, rapid geomorphing algorithm, which operates top down on a quadtree data structure." — [Röttger, Heidrich, Slusallek, Seidel, WSCG 1998, PDF](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- Röttger's geomorph interpolates only elevation, and the coarse value is the average of the two parents: "The elevation at these points is interpolated linearly with factor b between the elevation of the lower level (which is the average of the two corresponding corner points) and the elevation of the higher level. The latter is taken directly from height field data." The blend factor is stored as one byte: "we have 255 morphing steps, which is precise enough in practice." The paper never mentions normals, colours or Gouraud shading in the morph. — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- Röttger's only lighting remark is about silhouette-weighted error metrics: "more involved criteria such as silhouette tests cannot be included into this method, since they require the analysis of the whole data set for each view point. In practice, however, this is not too restrictive, because over-emphasizing silhouettes causes lighting artifacts for non-silhouette polygons." — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- de Boer 2000 (GeoMipMapping) states the Gouraud effect of a connectivity change explicitly, in the crack-fixing section: "A downside of this method is that, when changing connectivity of vertices, a slight change in (gouraud) shading will occur. This sometimes can be spotted in real-time, but it is only slightly noticable." — [de Boer, Fast Terrain Rendering Using Geometrical MipMapping, 2000, PDF](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)
- de Boer's geomorph ("trilinear GeoMipMapping") morphs only the odd vertices' heights: the fraction t between the two switch distances is "a multiplier ... for the white vertices of GeoMipMap level 0. This will slowly 'morph' level 0's geometry to level 1's until d has reached D1 ... This essentially replaces the sudden change (popping) of GeoMipMap levels with a smooth transition between both." Normals are not mentioned. — [de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)
- Strugar's CDLOD paper identifies vertex lighting as the thing that pops under discrete mesh swaps: "since the mesh swap between LOD levels happens between meshes of different triangle count and shape, differences in vertex output interpolation will result in 'popping' artifacts that appear if any vertex-based effect (such as vertex lighting) is used." CDLOD avoids it because "the algorithm used to transition between LOD levels completely transforms the mesh of a higher level into the lower detailed one before the actual swap occurs." — [Strugar, Continuous Distance-Dependent Level of Detail for Rendering Heightmaps, PDF](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/raw/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)
- Strugar's own demo does not light from vertex normals at all: "The datasets califmtns_large and hawaii are displayed using lighting based on one directional light and normals from the high resolution normalmap texture, with no overlay texturing. The puget dataset uses only overlay texture with baked lighting." — [Strugar CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/raw/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998 (view-dependent progressive meshes for terrain) morphs normals and colours along with position: "Besides position, other vertex attributes interpolated during a geomorph may include normal, color, and texture coordinates. Most attributes are linearly interpolated. Normals are interpolated over the unit sphere." — [Hoppe, Smooth view-dependent level-of-detail control and its application to terrain rendering, 1998, PDF](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998 also notes that if lighting is not per-vertex, vertex normals can be dropped altogether: "If in addition, texture mapping is performed without Gouraud shading, the storage of vertex normals becomes unnecessary." — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Hoppe/Asirvatham (GPU Gems 2, ch. 2, geometry clipmaps) blend both the height and the normal across the same transition region: "For smooth transitions, the vertex shader blends the geometry near the outer boundary of each level with that of the next-coarser level ... w is the transition width (chosen to be n/10)", and in the pixel shader: "For smooth shading transitions, the shader looks up the normals for the current level and the next-coarser level and blends them using the alpha value computed in the vertex shader." The normal map is stored at higher resolution than the geometry: "We let the normal map have twice the resolution of the geometry, because one normal per vertex is too blurry." — [Asirvatham & Hoppe, Terrain Rendering Using GPU-Based Geometry Clipmaps, GPU Gems 2, PDF](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)
- The clipmap normal for a level is computed from that level's own (filtered) elevation, not the finest: "The shader that updates the normal map for a level takes as input the elevation map at the same level. It computes the current normal as the cross product of two grid-aligned tangent vectors. Additionally, it does a texture lookup to gather the normal from the coarser level." — [GPU Gems 2 ch. 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)
- Unreal Engine 3 Landscape documentation, section "High Resolution LOD-Independent Lighting": "Since the X and Y slopes of the landscape are being stored, the entire high-resolution (non-LOD'd) normal data is available for lighting calculations. This makes it possible to always use the highest resolution of the terrain for per-pixel lighting even on distant components that have been LOD'd out." Geometry LOD is a mip-based morph: "both mip levels can be sampled and then the heights and X and Y offsets can be interpolated in the vertex shader creating a clean morphing effect." — [UDK Landscape (UE3) docs](https://docs.unrealengine.com/udk/Three/Landscape.html)
- UE3 Landscape Materials page (snippet): "from the May 2011 build onward, the geometry normal is sampled per-pixel automatically, so no extra material nodes are needed for LOD-independent high-resolution lighting"; the earlier workaround was to sample the heightmap texture in the material for "consistent, high resolution lighting even when the Landscape is LOD'd out in the distance." — [UDN LandscapeMaterials (snippet)](https://udn.epicgames.com/Three/LandscapeMaterials.html)
- UE4 Landscape technical page (snippet): render data is "packed into a 32-bit Texture with the height occupying 16-bits in the form of the R and G channels and the normal, stored as 2 8-bit values for X and Y, occupying the B and A channels". — [UE 4.26 Landscape docs (snippet)](https://docs.unrealengine.com/4.26/BuildingWorlds/Landscape)
- Unity's Terrain "Pixel Error" setting is documented purely as a geometric error budget: "Simplifies the generated terrain to optimize rendering. The lower the value, the more faithful the rendering to the original maps and the higher the performance needs." The older scripting reference defines heightmapPixelError as "An approximation of how many pixels the terrain will pop in the worst case when switching lod." Unity's manual says nothing about lighting across terrain LOD. — [Unity Manual, Terrain settings](https://docs.unity3d.com/Manual/terrain-OtherSettings.html); [Unity ScriptReference Terrain.heightmapPixelError](https://docs.unity3d.com/ScriptReference/Terrain-heightmapPixelError.html)
- Wagner, "Terrain Geomorphing in the Vertex Shader" (ShaderX2), builds on de Boer and abandons vertex normals (snippet): "The number of vertices of a patch changes during rendering (see view-dependent tessellation) which forbids using vertex normals for lighting. Therefore a lightmap ... is used instead." and "Since the terrain's triangulation changes over time, vertex normals cannot be used for lighting. Instead a pre-calculated lightmap is used." — [Wagner, TU Wien publication page (snippet)](https://www.ims.tuwien.ac.at/publications/tuw-138077.pdf); [gamedev.net mirror (snippet)](https://www.gamedev.net/tutorials/programming/graphics/terrain-geomorphing-in-the-vertex-shader-r1936)
- A gamedev.net thread on large-terrain popping and normals (snippet): neighbouring meshes at different LODs have normals pointing "in slightly different directions, causing lighting seams"; the poster argues normals need interpolating along with vertex positions, says precomputed normals reused across all LODs "looks like crap", and notes "The normals I'm sampling at lower LOD are also biased towards the flat ground normals farther away, since the triangles themselves are larger." — [gamedev.net topic 688460 (snippet)](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/688460-large-terrain-rendering-reducing-popping-and-calculating-normals/)
- A Khronos forum reply on why coarse terrain shades differently (snippet): "When you bring the terrain down to a lower polygon count you have less vertices to work with. As a result, you lose alot of the finer detail in the shading"; a normal map avoids most of that loss. — [Khronos community: terrain optimization (snippet)](https://community.khronos.org/t/terrain-optimization/46569)
- vterrain.org's implementation survey lists, as the advice in Seumas Young's terrain tutorial (Lindstrom-derived LOD), "bake the lighting into the terrain, don't recompute detail every frame, limit camera motion, etc." — [vterrain.org LOD Implementations](http://vterrain.org/LOD/Implementations/)

### Inferences
- Inference: a normal computed from the fine heightfield at vertex creation gives the *fine-level* shade immediately, which is by construction not equal to the colour Gouraud was interpolating along the parent edge; so remedy (1) on its own replaces a geometric pop with a shading pop of the same timing. None of the sources claim otherwise; Hoppe's clipmap explicitly blends from the coarse normal to the fine normal over the transition for this reason.
- Inference from Röttger's morph rule ("the lower level ... is the average of the two corresponding corner points"): the colour analogue is to give a new midpoint vertex the average of its two parents' Gouraud colours (or normals) at creation and then blend toward its fine-heightfield colour with the same byte factor b. On the split edge, a Gouraud rasteriser's interpolated colour at the midpoint is exactly the average of the endpoint colours, so this is pixel-identical on that edge at b = 0; across the fan interior the new diagonal changes interpolation slightly (de Boer's "slight change in (gouraud) shading" case). This is the CPU-side equivalent of Hoppe 1998's "normal, color ... interpolated during a geomorph".
- Inference: of the five remedies in the brief, (3) baked lightmap/normal map and (5) lighting LOD independent of geometry (Unreal's stored slopes) are the modern-engine standard and both require texture-space lighting, which the brief says this engine lacks. (2) morphing normals/colours with heights (Hoppe 1998, clipmaps) and (1)/(5)-at-vertex-level (a precomputed per-grid-point normal or colour table indexed by heightfield coordinates, so every LOD's vertex at grid cell (i,j) always gets the same normal regardless of which level created it) are feasible at vertex-creation time on the CPU with the full heightfield in memory. (4) flat shading per tile is not described by any source found as a 1990s or modern remedy for terrain; de Boer's and Strugar's demos use smooth shading or textures.
- Inference: the gamedev.net poster's "normals at lower LOD are biased towards flat" is the mechanism behind "new vertices change the look of a flat area": a coarse-level normal from large triangles is an average slope, so a flat-looking coarse area that contains sub-cell relief gets visibly rougher shading when the fine normals arrive. A per-grid-point normal table computed once from the 5 m heightfield (with a chosen filter width) would make the coarse and fine vertices agree in shade; what remains is only the interpolation-geometry change de Boer calls slight.

### Gaps
- No paper or engine doc was found that measures whether fine-heightfield normals at every level reduce the perceived pop versus coarse-level normals; the claim is only argued (gamedev.net thread, Khronos thread) and never quantified.
- Lindstrom 1996 ("Real-time, continuous level of detail rendering of height fields") could not be fetched (TLS/redirect failures on both gatech hosts), so its wording on shading across LOD is not confirmed here.
- Unity's current terrain documentation says nothing about normals or lighting across LOD; whether Unity's terrain shader samples a full-resolution terrain normal map independent of geometry LOD could not be confirmed from docs reached.
- The Wagner article and gamedev.net thread were not readable in full (403 / JS-rendered); their quotes are from search snippets.

## Key question A2: Which remedies were used in 1990s games, which in modern engines, and which could be done at vertex-creation time on the CPU?

### Takeaway
1990s terrain renderers that are documented either shaded per vertex and accepted the change (de Boer's GeoMipMapping, 2000, says the Gouraud change is "only slightly noticeable"), or avoided per-vertex lighting entirely by baking shade into the colour map (NovaLogic's voxel engines, Hoppe's and Strugar's "baked lighting" datasets). Modern engines bake or decouple: Unreal samples full-resolution stored slopes per pixel; clipmaps use a 2x-resolution normal map blended across the transition; Strugar lights from a high-resolution normal map. The CPU-at-vertex-creation feasible set is: a per-grid-point normal/colour table (lighting consistent across levels), and morphing the colour/normal with the existing height blend factor.

### Cited Findings
- NovaLogic's Voxel Space (Comanche, Delta Force) carried shading in the colour map and never lit at run time (snippet of the open-source reimplementation's README): the engine used "a 1024 x 1024 one-byte height map and a 1024 x 1024 one-byte color map"; because the colour map "already held shading and shadows, the engine took the color directly and didn't need to compute illumination during rendering." — [s-macke/VoxelSpace README (snippet)](https://github.com/s-macke/VoxelSpace/blob/add-license-1/README.md)
- Delta Force (1998) "used the Voxel Space engine, known from earlier games such as the Comanche series, which uses voxels to visualise terrain while polygons are used for rendering characters, vehicles, buildings and other details"; the engine "did not support any form of 3D acceleration". Delta Force 2's "Voxel Space 32" added hardware acceleration. — [Wikipedia: Delta Force (video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Force_(video_game)); [Wikipedia: Delta Force 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Force_2)
- Magic Carpet (Bullfrog, 1994) used Gouraud-shaded polygon terrain: an early build "temporarily featured blue terrain to assist with Gouraud Shaded lighting effects"; the engine "was designed as a 'vertical scanning voxelish engine', but was rewritten by the end of the project to be an affine texture mapper." — [The Cutting Room Floor: Prerelease: Magic Carpet (DOS)](https://new.tcrf.net/Prerelease:Magic_Carpet_(DOS))
- MechWarrior 2: Mercenaries "still used the MechWarrior 2 engine but with upgraded graphics, with terrain texture mapping, enhanced lighting effects and higher resolutions" (search summary of the Wikipedia article). — [Wikipedia: MechWarrior 2: Mercenaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MechWarrior_2:_Mercenaries)
- A 2026 retrospective states the 1995 MechWarrior 2 engine "was reused for Interstate '76, Heavy Gear, and Battlezone" (retrospective's claim; not verified against a primary source, and the Battlezone attribution is doubtful). — [generationamiga.com MechWarrior 2 retrospective](https://www.generationamiga.com/2026/07/25/mechwarrior-2-retrospective-review-why-it-became-a-pc-gaming-milestone/)
- Hoppe/Asirvatham clipmaps: normal map updated on the GPU from the level's elevation, blended fine-to-coarse in the transition region; "one normal per vertex is too blurry" so the normal map is at twice the geometry resolution. — [GPU Gems 2 ch. 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)
- Unreal Landscape: full-resolution X/Y slopes stored with the height so "the entire high-resolution (non-LOD'd) normal data is available for lighting calculations". — [UDK Landscape docs](https://docs.unrealengine.com/udk/Three/Landscape.html)
- Wagner (ShaderX2, 2003): triangulation changes "forbids using vertex normals for lighting. Therefore a lightmap ... is used instead" (snippet). — [TU Wien tuw-138077 (snippet)](https://www.ims.tuwien.ac.at/publications/tuw-138077.pdf)
- Strugar's puget dataset "uses only overlay texture with baked lighting"; the others light per pixel "from the high resolution normalmap texture". — [Strugar CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/raw/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998 geomorphs "may include normal, color, and texture coordinates ... Normals are interpolated over the unit sphere." — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998's terrain examples drop vertex normals when Gouraud is not used ("the storage of vertex normals becomes unnecessary"), i.e. the 1998 terrain flyover is textured without per-vertex lighting. — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Outcast (1999) hid far-field artefacts with blur rather than lighting tricks: "It just made stuff in the distance look blurry", "covering the voxel jaggies on buildings in the distance"; the game "was software-only" and ran at most at "512x384". — [Shamus Young, Outcast: Something Different](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=890)

### Inferences
- Inference: the documented 1990s pattern is "shade in the colour data, not in the geometry" (voxel engines) or "accept the slight Gouraud change" (de Boer); nobody documented a per-tile flat-shading fallback for terrain.
- Inference: given the brief's constraints (vertex write hook, full 5 m heightfield resident, no texture-space lighting), the two implementable options are: (i) a one-time per-grid-point normal (or final Gouraud colour, if the light is static per mission) table over the full heightfield, read by whichever level creates the vertex; this is the vertex-level equivalent of Unreal's "LOD-independent lighting"; (ii) on creation, start the vertex at the average of its parents' colours and lerp to the table value with the same b used for height. (i) removes level-dependence of shade; (ii) removes the time-discontinuity at creation. Both are O(1) per vertex.
- Inference: Hoppe's "one normal per vertex is too blurry" and the gamedev.net "biased towards flat" remark point the same way: whichever normal table is used, its filter width decides whether far coarse areas look flat or textured; a table computed at 5 m spacing will look rougher at 1800 m than the coarse-level normals do today, so the filter width is a tunable, not a given.

### Gaps
- No source describes the lighting path of Interstate '76 / MechWarrior 2 beyond the brief; nothing about I'76's terrain shading was found on community pages (see B4).
- No source was found for Starsiege: Tribes' or MechWarrior 3's terrain lighting or LOD (several searches, nothing technical).

## Key question B1: Would a coarser far setting, or one fixed coarse level beyond N metres, look better or worse than rings of increasing detail; how does a coarse plane misrepresent ridges; what do silhouette-aware error metrics say; was "detail within N m, one coarse level beyond" ever recommended, and with what artefact?

### Takeaway
The literature's consistent position is that distance-only block LOD (which "detail within N m, coarse beyond" is the degenerate case of) produces fixed-distance transition lines that move with the camera, and that popping is governed by projected error in pixels plus local roughness, not by distance alone. Röttger's roughness propagation exists precisely so that isolated peaks in flat ground stay finely triangulated at any distance; ROAM and Hoppe add silhouette/normal terms; Röttger admits image quality "is worst at the silhouettes" but argues geometry jumps matter more to the eye. No source recommends a single coarse far level; de Boer's texture-mipmap analogy names the artefact ("borders ... at fixed distances from the camera").

### Cited Findings
- de Boer on fixed-distance switching: "Using a preset (fixed) d for each GeoMipMap level will result in unwanted popping artifacts. Switching from one level to another at a fixed d without any condition will result in a sudden change in geometry". — [de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)
- de Boer on the visible band at a level boundary (the texture-mipmap analogy he applies to geometry): "When looking at ordinary texture mipmapping, one can clearly see the borders of where two mipmaps of different levels touch eachother. These borders, or lines if you will, occur at fixed distances from the camera and can be eliminated by using Trilinear filtering. ... This same principle can be applied to GeoMipMaps." — [de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)
- de Boer on the error threshold vs detail trade: "Although setting tau to 1 or less will eliminate popping altogether, it also implies that the probability of using a higher level GeoMipMap (lower detail) will be very low. A certain value of tau has to be found which gives a good balance". — [de Boer 2000](https://www.flipcode.com/archives/article_geomipmaps.pdf)
- Röttger criticises per-block distance LOD: "this multi-resolution strategy is not optimal, since it is assumed that the distance to the point of view is constant throughout each block." His criterion combines distance and pre-computed roughness: "Vertex removal is performed depending on its distance to the point of view as well as local surface roughness, which is pre-calculated." — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- Röttger's roughness propagation is designed to keep isolated hills: Figure 8 caption, "Propagating d2-values causes finer triangulation near local peaks in a flat surface", with the text "Here a few small peaks are placed on an otherwise flat surface." — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- Röttger on silhouettes vs pops: "The image quality is worst at the silhouettes. The human eye, however, is more sensitive to sudden changes of geometry, which have been significantly reduced by the geomorphing algorithm." — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- Röttger's operating point: "The quality control C was set to a value of 8 ... The value of c was dynamically chosen as to maintain a fixed frame rate of 25 Hertz, which resulted in approximately 1600 triangle fans, or a total of roughly 15000 vertices per frame." — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- ROAM (Duchaineau et al. 1997) lists silhouettes as an error-metric term: "Silhouette edges: Specific emphasis can be placed on triangles whose normal bounds indicate potential back-face to front-face transitions." Its base metric is screen-space distortion with "guaranteed error bounds". — [ROAM paper, PDF](https://classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2003/fall/23700-1/docs/roam.pdf)
- ROAM's reported point where pops vanish: "In a high-speed terrain-following scenario over rough terrain with 3000 terrain triangles in the mesh, silhouettes appear realistically complex and stable and there are virtually no popping artifacts. In a similar flight over less rough but still mountainous terrain 1500 triangles suffice to eliminate perceptible popping." — [ROAM](https://classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2003/fall/23700-1/docs/roam.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998 found that backface (away-facing) simplification bought nothing on terrain and dropped it: "Experiments reveal that backface simplification presents little benefit. For instance, in Figure 11 it does not result in any coarsening of the mesh. We therefore omit it as a view-dependent refinement criterion". — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998 on why a naive error estimate pops: "an initial implementation using the naive approximation error did in fact result in unexpectedly large pops ... The solution is to measure maximum (L-inf) approximation error with respect to a reference surface." — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Strugar's CDLOD range design: "distances covered by each LOD layer are precalculated ... with the goal of producing approximately the same average number of triangles per square unit of screen over the whole rendered terrain"; "the range of each level being two times larger than that of the previous one, and the range of the final level (largest, least detailed) representing the required total viewing distance"; "This morph area usually covers the last 15%-30% of every LOD range." — [Strugar CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/raw/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)
- Strugar on shrinking the morph band: "The size of the morph area can also be decreased to mitigate this problem, but that can make the transition between levels noticeable." — [Strugar CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/raw/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)
- Geometry clipmaps are explicitly rings of halving resolution around the viewer with a transition band of n/10 at each ring's outer edge: "we extract a set of L nested 'rings' centered about the viewer. The finest-level ring is filled in." and "w is the transition width (chosen to be n/10)". — [GPU Gems 2 ch. 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)
- A 2026 clipmap write-up (snippet) describes the practical test for ring artefacts: static screenshots hid transition artefacts, while "constant-speed camera traversals across ring boundaries exposed them"; it puts the geomorph zone at "roughly the outer 20% of each ring". — [cinevva.com, open-world browser part 06: clipmaps (snippet)](https://app.cinevva.com/blog/2026-02-25-open-world-browser-part-06-clipmaps)
- The I26 Godot reimplementation of Interstate '76 uses a coarse "distant mountain ring" under/beyond the map in its modern look and had exactly the coarse-plane-vs-real-ground artefact: "Modern look: flat-shaded, low-poly terrain poking through the real ground near the map's edge (cliff walls, canyon rims). The distant mountain ring runs under the map as a backstop, and its coarse cells at the map's edge could stand above the modern [ground]" (v0.6.0 fixed list). — [I26 release v0.6.0](https://github.com/vahokif/I26/releases/tag/v0.6.0)
- I26's 1997-look far mode showed a moving, blocky band where the coarse horizon met the ground: "1997 look at Far: the sky's horizon mountains broke into vertical blocks at different heights that jumped up and down as you drove (Seminole shows it well). Their foot followed where the ground ends one degree at a time, measured again every 60 m; it now runs smoothly along that end and moves with the eye." — [I26 release v0.7.0](https://github.com/vahokif/I26/releases/tag/v0.7.0)

### Inferences
- Inference: "detail within N m, one coarse level beyond" is de Boer's "preset (fixed) d" case with a single switch; the artefact he names (a border line "at fixed distances from the camera") would sit at N and travel with the player. Rings with geomorph bands (Strugar 15-30 %, clipmaps 10 %, cinevva 20 %) exist to dissolve that line; a single far level has one line instead of several, but nothing in the sources says one large line is less visible than several small ones (see B2).
- Inference on ridges: a coarse far level built by subsampling (de Boer's GeoMipMap levels, Röttger's parent averages) drops any feature narrower than its cell; Röttger's d2 propagation and ROAM's silhouette term are the two documented ways to keep a far hill's silhouette in the mesh at a coarse distance. With a pure distance-only far level there is no such term, so a ridge crest between coarse samples disappears from the horizon line ("the hill behind the barn missing"); this is the mechanism, though no source uses that phrase.
- Inference: at 1800-2400 m with a 32,767-vertex budget, the question is where the vertices buy the most: Röttger ran about 15,000 vertices/frame for a 1025x1025 field at 25 Hz with C = 8 and a frame-rate-driven c; the retrofit has roughly twice that, which suggests a roughness-aware metric (keeping peaks) rather than a flat far plane is affordable.

### Gaps
- No source was found that makes the comparative claim "one coarse far level looks better/worse than rings" in those words; the judgement above is inferred from de Boer's mipmap-border analogy and Strugar's morph-band remarks.
- Lindstrom 1996's screen-space metric and its silhouette discussion could not be fetched.
- No 1990s game source documents a "one coarse level beyond N m" terrain design; the 1990s material found (voxel engines, Outcast, Magic Carpet) describes draw-distance/fog cut-offs, not two-tier LOD.

## Key question B2: Is there any measurement or statement that fewer, larger detail steps are more or less visible than many small ones, and what is the perceptual argument (size of change on screen per frame vs count)?

### Takeaway
No study comparing "few large steps" against "many small steps" for terrain was found. The literature contains both halves of the argument: Hoppe 1998 reports that a projected error of a few pixels becomes "nearly imperceptible" when spread over frames by geomorphs (size per frame), while ROAM attributes its low popping partly to making "very few mesh changes per frame" (count). Scherzer et al. state that any discrete switch is usually perceptible however small, and Reddy's thesis puts LOD thresholds on object screen size, angular velocity and eccentricity.

### Cited Findings
- Hoppe 1998: "Although the model may at times have a projected geometric error of a few pixels, results indicate that geomorphs make this error nearly imperceptible ... The geomorph scheme is effective enough that we can increase the pixel error tolerance to improve frame rate (up to 72 frames/sec) with few noticeable artifacts." The geomorphs "have a lifetime of one second". — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- Hoppe 1998 on what still shows through: "Even though geomorphs hide such pops, it is still useful to have an accurate estimate of screen-space error." — [Hoppe 1998](https://hhoppe.com/svdlod.pdf)
- ROAM, feature 11: "Reduced 'popping': ROAM's use of a screen-distortion metric and its tendency to make very few mesh changes per frame naturally reduces disturbing temporal artifacts (pops). In some cases it is desirable to reduce pops further by vertex [morphing]". — [ROAM](https://classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2003/fall/23700-1/docs/roam.pdf)
- Scherzer & Wimmer 2008 on discrete switches: "switching from one representation to another can lead to noticeable popping artifacts. In practice, even if the difference between two LOD levels is very small, switching is usually perceptible, because a pixel-exact match does not occur." — [Scherzer, Wimmer, Frame Sequential Interpolation for Discrete Level-of-Detail Rendering, 2008 draft PDF](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2008/SCHERZER-2008-FSR/SCHERZER-2008-FSR-draft.pdf)
- Reddy 1997 (PhD, Edinburgh) bases LOD on perceivable spatial frequency "taking into consideration the size of an object, its angular velocity, and the degree to which it exists in the peripheral field", and reports that with size-based thresholds "the discrepancies are only in the order of 1 to 2 pixels: around 0.2% of the display resolution ... the author can attest that no significant or distracting popping effects were experienced." It also notes distance-based thresholds "are no longer valid and must also be scaled appropriately" when display resolution changes. — [Reddy, Perceptually Modulated Level of Detail for Virtual Environments, 1997, PDF](https://www.dcs.ed.ac.uk/home/mxr/thesis/thesis.pdf)
- Schwarz & Stamminger 2009 model popping visibility with "a spatio-velocity color vision model" and say "Popping is a major source of visual artifacts in dynamic scenes ... We first discuss several issues affecting popping perception, pointing out its complexity." — [Schwarz & Stamminger, On predicting visual popping in dynamic scenes, APGV 2009](https://cris.fau.de/converis/portal/publication/118172384?lang=en_GB)
- Röttger: "The human eye ... is more sensitive to sudden changes of geometry" than to static silhouette error. — [Röttger 1998](https://dspace.zcu.cz/bitstream/11025/15845/1/Slusallek_98.pdf)
- Unity documents its terrain threshold as a worst-case pop size in pixels: "An approximation of how many pixels the terrain will pop in the worst case when switching lod." — [Unity Terrain.heightmapPixelError](https://docs.unity3d.com/ScriptReference/Terrain-heightmapPixelError.html)
- Blender's game engine added LOD hysteresis (default 10 %) "to avoid popping when a LOD object moves close to the LOD transition continuously" (snippet of commit message). — [Blender game engine source archive (snippet)](https://projects.blender.org/archive/blender-archive/src/commit/1b71279adccb9f8cc1f4d7cbb3cbb06871da982e/source/gameengine)
- Unigine docs (snippet): discrete LODs "are likely to show 'popping', when one LOD is switched to another", and blending over a fade range makes the transition imperceptible. — [Unigine World Management (snippet)](https://developer.unigine.com/en/docs/2.9/principles/world_management/)

### Inferences
- Inference: the sources support "a change is noticed by its projected size per frame" (Hoppe: a few pixels spread over a one-second geomorph is nearly invisible; Reddy: 1-2 pixel discrepancies not distracting; Unity's threshold is a pixel count) and separately "count matters" (ROAM). Reconciling them: Scherzer says any instantaneous switch is perceptible, so fewer-but-larger instantaneous steps are not predicted to be better; the only documented route to invisibility is making each step's per-frame screen motion small (geomorph), after which the count of simultaneous small changes is what ROAM credits.
- Inference: Reddy's resolution remark applies directly to the retrofit: a pop that was sub-threshold at 640x480 is 3x larger in pixels at 1440p, and a 1800 m view versus 600 m moves every transition ring outward, so thresholds tuned for 1997 do not carry over.

### Gaps
- No psychophysical study comparing step size against step frequency for terrain LOD was found (searches on "LOD popping perception magnitude frequency", "fade vs discrete LOD perception").
- Reddy's thesis was searched only for "popping" and the abstract; its experiments concern object LOD, not terrain, and were not read in full.
- Schwarz & Stamminger's full text (the factors they found to govern popping visibility) was not obtained; only the abstract page.

## Key question B3: The role of fog/haze in hiding LOD transitions in the 1990s and in modern engines, and whether a soft haze band at the far rings is a legitimate cheap remedy at 1800 m

### Takeaway
Fog was the standard 1990s mask for the clipping plane (Wikipedia) and remains a documented LOD mask in a modern terrain engine: Cesium raises the screen-space error of tiles partly in fog and culls tiles fully in fog, with the warning that aggressive fog makes tile popping *more* noticeable. For Interstate '76 specifically, the I26 project records the original's 600 m clip and its own "Far" mode drawing to 20 km with "plain sky and haze" and a distant mountain ring; nothing more specific about the original's fog was found.

### Cited Findings
- Wikipedia on 1990s fogging: distance fog was needed "when processing power was not enough to render far viewing distances"; "Clipping could be very distracting since bits and pieces of polygons would flicker in and out of view instantly"; fogged polygons "were obscured by the fog, fading in as the player approached". — [Wikipedia: Distance fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_fog)
- Wikipedia on pop-in (snippet): developers often hide a short draw distance by obscuring the area with a distance fog; racing games were especially prone because open roads led to "pop-up graphics". — [Wikipedia: Draw distance (snippet)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draw_distance)
- Cesium (2015), a modern terrain engine: "Early games introduced fog to hide artifacts of geometry rendered in the distance"; fog is kept because "it adds realism to the scene and reduces the number of draw calls since geometry completely in fog can be culled"; "Terrain tiles that would be partially in fog will have their screen-space error increased"; "Terrain tiles that are completely in the fog will be culled completely or not even requested"; measured effect: rendered tiles 327 to 228, requested 510 to 322, 115 to 155 fps for horizon views; and the caveat "Changing the parameters to make the fog more aggressive may cause a more noticeable 'popping' of the terrain tiles." — [Cesium blog: Graphics Tech in Cesium - Fog](https://cesium.com/blog/2015/11/12/fog/)
- Outcast (1999) used a depth-of-field blur rather than fog as its far-field mask: "It just made stuff in the distance look blurry", "covering the voxel jaggies on buildings in the distance". — [Shamus Young on Outcast](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=890)
- Terminal Velocity's 2010s "Boosted Edition" is described by a GOG user review (snippet) as having changed "the draw distance and fog of war ... so you can see farther, so it feels less claustrophobic" (player impression of a re-release; not evidence about the 1995 renderer). — [GOG: Terminal Velocity Boosted Edition (snippet)](https://www.gog.com/game/terminal_velocity_boosted_edition)
- I26 (Godot reimplementation of Interstate '76) documents the original's cut-off and its own far mode: "Draw Distance (Graphic Detail): Original clips at the original's 600 m in either look; Far, the default, draws to 20 km as before." — [I26 release v0.4.0](https://github.com/vahokif/I26/releases/tag/v0.4.0)
- I26's modern look pairs a coarse mountain ring with haze beyond the map: Nitro arenas with no horizon panels "showed a white band where the mountains should be ... the modern look now draws the plain sky and haze there, and the mountains of Far are unchanged." — [I26 release v0.7.0](https://github.com/vahokif/I26/releases/tag/v0.7.0)
- I26 on the original's horizon treatment: "The modern look's distant mountains sit as low as the original's on high maps" and, for night missions in the 1997 look, "horizon panels sunk as the original's". — [I26 release v0.3.0](https://github.com/vahokif/I26/releases/tag/v0.3.0)
- I26 on the 1997 CD software renderer's sky: "the sky draws as the 1997 CD's software mode drew it, with a colour gradient along the horizon in place of the mountain panels the later releases added. The band rises about twelve degrees over the horizon, brightest at its foot, in the mission palette's own sky blues." — [I26 release v0.7.0](https://github.com/vahokif/I26/releases/tag/v0.7.0)
- Hoppe's clipmap paper and Strugar's paper never mention fog as part of the LOD scheme (full-text search: no "fog" hits in either). — [GPU Gems 2 ch. 2](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf); [Strugar CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/raw/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)

### Inferences
- Inference: a soft haze band starting inside the outermost ring is consistent with both the 1990s practice (fog at the clip) and Cesium's modern practice (fog-raised error tolerance in the far tiles), and it is cheap because the engine already has a per-vertex fog term for the 600 m case. Cesium's caveat transfers: haze lowers the contrast of the far pop but does not remove it, and if haze is used to justify a coarser far level the pop under the haze grows; the net is only favourable if haze density at the ring radius is high enough that the per-frame change is below contrast threshold (Reddy's framework), which no source quantifies for this case.
- Inference: because the retrofit's rings would be at roughly 3-4x the original fog radius, a haze that matches the original's look at 600 m would have to be much thinner, so it masks less per metre; the far rings then need their own morph band (Strugar's 15-30 % of range) rather than relying on haze alone.

### Gaps
- Nothing was found on the original Interstate '76's fog implementation, colour or curve beyond "600 m" (brief and I26 v0.4.0 note); no Local Ditch or interstate76.com page about fog, draw distance or "Far" came up in any search (the "Local Ditch" queries returned only unrelated road/weather pages).
- No source quantifies how much fog density is needed to hide a given screen-space pop.
